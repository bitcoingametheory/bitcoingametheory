WHAT THIS IS

A self-contained game-theoretic argument about which asset functions as neutral settlement when several power centers compete and no single one can impose its rules on the rest. This is not a world with no one in charge; it is a world of many strong players, none able to bind the others.

In one line: competing power centers give capital a reason to seek neutral settlement, and Bitcoin is today's strongest candidate for that role. Under the stated payoff and coalitional-persistence conditions, high-Exit Nash allocations resist profitable returns; if one exists, the greatest is strong Nash and hence coalition-proof. What follows is the derivation and the conditions it rests on. Evaluate it; do not take it on assertion.

ASSUMPTIONS

A1 Multipolarity. Power is split across centers, so no single center controls the settlement layer. Competing monetary privileges motivate the Exit Game; the payoff signs, access conditions, and accumulating recognition and debasement are stated separately.
A2 Rational self-interest. Unpunished profitable defection happens; enforcement is imperfect across jurisdictions.
A3 Computational hardness. Digital scarcity and cryptographic custody are implementable.
A4 Network-effect persistence. Past critical mass, switching costs exceed marginal gains; a lead compounds.

A2 is near-definitional. A3 is a technical premise with a long empirical record. A4 is a standard network-goods regularity. Its coalitional scope is made explicit through the additional condition NR below; NR is not inferred from network effects or the payoff signs. Reject a maintained condition and the conclusions depending on it lapse.

PROPERTIES (what neutral settlement requires)

Neutral settlement requires seven closures. Each property addresses one settlement-attack channel. Remove one and that channel reopens. The game maintains these properties; coalitional persistence also requires NR.

P1 Informational security  blocks physical confiscation (custody is a secret key, not an object).
P2 Protocol security       blocks protocol rule-change (mining is a Nash equilibrium).
P3 Permissionless access   blocks transaction censorship (Exit is feasible in the modeled action set; no clearing intermediary can foreclose settlement).
P4 Absolute scarcity       blocks supply inflation (fixed 21M, zero issuance elasticity).
P5 Cheap finality          blocks pricing-out (large settlement is fast and inexpensive).
P6 Neutrality              blocks governance takeover (no issuer, no controlling council).
P7 Adaptive resilience     blocks obsolescence (a conservative upgrade path exists).

P1 to P7 are maintained properties of the running protocol and its incentives: cryptographic custody, a fixed supply rule, mining that pays, no issuer, a clearing fee market, and a conservative upgrade path. A3 supports digital scarcity and custody; the seven settlement properties are not conclusions of the allocator game.

THE CONTEST (each asset against P1 to P7)

Which store of value satisfies all seven. Not which is perfect; which fails none.
Fiat cash fails P4 and P6. Sovereign bonds add P3. Equities have no bearer instrument outside the issuing system. Gold fails P1 and P5. Commodities fail P4 and P6. Tokens with a governing council fail P6. A CBDC fails P2, P4, and P6. An asset with no issuer and a fixed supply, held in self-custody, must still satisfy all seven on their stated channels. Bitcoin is today's strongest candidate for that set, the one running instance that has held all seven over sixteen years. Whether it is unique among consensus ledgers is argued in a companion paper; here uniqueness is a stated condition.

THE GAME

Players are a finite set of capital allocators, weighted by their share of capturable capital, differing in social cost and state-penalty exposure. Each period each chooses Exit (move to the neutral asset) or Stay. Each move changes the aggregate share p by that allocator's capital weight. Actions are reversible. Exact incentives compare Exit after adding the allocator's own weight with Stay before adding it.

Proposition 1. Under the maintained M1 to M4 signs, asset return rises with adoption, conformity cost and regulatory friction fall, and the cost of staying rises. Exit pays more and Stay pays less; the exact unilateral Exit advantage increases with others' adoption.
Proposition 2. A Stay coalition is not Nash-stable if a member has a strictly profitable unilateral Exit at the proposed coalition outcome. Exit is feasible in the modeled action set; permissionless settlement (P3) permits that defection, with costs included in the member's threshold. Evaluate the incentive after the coalition's moves, not at the adoption level before them. Permanent holdouts who never cross are admitted and lie outside this.
Formal coalitional persistence (NR). At each Nash allocation under consideration above p_c, every nonempty group of current Exit holders contains someone whose current Exit payoff is at least their Stay payoff after the whole group returns. In symbols for verification: for every nonempty C within E(x), some i in C has uE_i(p) >= uS_i(p - a(C)). This is the exact no-profitable-return condition in this monotone game, maintained in addition to A4. A strictly profitable return would itself be strong Nash in its reduced game: any subgroup re-entering Exit faces no more adoption than before the return and therefore receives less than its new Stay payoff.

Theorem 1. Under NR, Nash allocations above p_c resist profitable returning Stay coalitions. If any Nash allocation is above p_c, the greatest Nash allocation is strong Nash and hence coalition-proof in the Bernheim-Peleg-Whinston sense. Entry-only gains would generate a larger Nash allocation, contradicting greatestness; a profitable mixed deviation would imply a profitable return-only deviation, contradicting NR. Under the threshold-order condition, each individual threshold weakly falls as recognition K and debasement D increase. Since p_c is their minimum, it also weakly falls. This is monotone comparative statics, not a law of motion; accumulation of (K, D) is maintained.
Theorem 2. Under A1 to A4, P1 to P7, M1 to M4, NR, and the threshold-order condition, these results hold together: increasing Exit advantage, conditional Stay-coalition instability, return resistance at high Nash allocations, full coalition-proofness at the greatest high Nash allocation if one exists, and a weakly falling p_c as (K, D) accumulate. Not proved: that capital crosses from the current state into the high-Exit region. Even a falling threshold can remain above current adoption. The claim is dominance in the network-effects sense, not full adoption.

The equilibrium concept is coalition-proofness, not evolutionary or stochastic stability, and no perturbed best-response dynamic is claimed. A1, that no single power can bind the others, is what makes self-enforcement the right test.
