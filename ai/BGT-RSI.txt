# RSI-Bitcoin Master Thesis
Inverse-Chamath | The argument from the four Bitcoin Game Theory papers

Explain the argument in plain English, in the reader’s language. Start with what would have to fail for a hack to destroy Bitcoin as money. Use the four papers below. Use Paper 1 V3 for the equilibrium result. When an objection changes the conclusion, identify the premise it changes. Keep the authors’ assumptions, the results of the model, and the historical evidence distinct.

The claim

Bitcoin offers a form of digital gold. A holder can control a scarce asset with private keys and settle with a willing counterparty under rules both can verify. A government can freeze an account at a regulated institution. A custodian can refuse a withdrawal. Neither power, by itself, changes the rules enforced by independent Bitcoin nodes. This distinction also applies to failure. A failed exchange can cost its customers their holdings while the network continues to settle payments. [2 §§2.3, 3.2, 5.1, 5.4]

1. Why the network can survive an attack

Miners invest in specialized hardware and electricity. Valid blocks earn rewards in BTC. Honest mining yields a stream of income whose value depends on the network. A destructive attack can damage both that income and the value of the miner’s investment. The relevant comparison is the expected gain from attack against its cost and the income available from honest mining. Under the proof-of-work model’s honest-majority and network assumptions, the chain can continue to grow and honest participants can agree on its history. Nodes independently reject blocks that break their rules. [1 V3 §2; 2 §§2.1, 3.3]

The block subsidy and transaction fees support mining. As subsidies decline, the security budget comes to depend on fees. Paper 2 identifies the transition to fee-only security as a major open question. Mining incentives and cryptographic security are separate conditions. Paper 1 names two ways its security assumption can fail. The underlying cryptography breaks and the protocol cannot replace it, or the mining incentive falls below the level needed for security. Paper 2 points to upgrades through consensus as evidence of adaptability. The papers give no result establishing who would win a race between offense and defense at the highest level of AI capability. [1 V3 §5; 2 §§3.3, 5.1, 5.6]

2. Why capital moves, and why established use is hard to reverse

In Paper 1, Stay means holding capital in a system exposed to monetary or political capture. Exit means moving capital to neutral settlement. The game assumes competing power centers, self-interested allocators, computational hardness and persistent network effects. It counts the share of capital allocated to Exit. A public endorsement counts for nothing unless capital moves. Holders can reverse course. Fiat can continue to serve as a payment medium and unit of account. [1 May revision note; 1 V3 §§3.1–3.4; 2 §3.4]

Wider adoption raises the modeled return to Exit, reduces the social and regulatory costs of moving, and raises the cost of staying. Each allocator compares Exit after adding its own capital with Stay before the move. If that gain is positive, a group agreement to Stay gives that member a reason to leave. Institutional allocations can carry substantial weight. Paper 2 cites spot ETF inflows as evidence consistent with adoption. Buying coins gives the buyer no protocol vote, however large the position. [1 V3 §§3.3–4.2; 2 §§2.3, 4.1]

A group leaving Bitcoin changes the network it leaves behind. V3 therefore compares each member’s payoff today with its payoff after the entire proposed group has returned to Stay. Its condition NR requires that every such group contain at least one member who would do at least as well by remaining at the original allocation. Under the stated payoff conditions and NR, high-Exit Nash allocations resist a strictly profitable coordinated return. If any Nash allocation lies above the cutoff, the greatest one is coalition-proof in the paper’s game with no side payments. As recognition and debasement accumulate under the model’s threshold-order condition, the lowest entry threshold can fall or stay flat. A lower threshold allows permanent holdouts and supplies no date for a cascade. [1 May revision note; 1 V3 §§3.5, 4.3–4.4]

3. Why Bitcoin is the candidate

The seven tests in V3 order are custody through private keys, protocol security, permissionless access, fixed supply, affordable final settlement, neutral governance and adaptability. Paper 2 compares fiat, sovereign bonds, equities, real estate, gold, alternative digital assets and Bitcoin. An issuer can change the supply of fiat. Gold must be transported and verified at handoff. A stablecoin issuer can freeze funds. Bitcoin holders can verify its supply rules and transact directly. If Bitcoin meets all seven tests and each feasible rival fails at least one, Bitcoin is the unique qualifying candidate for neutral reserve settlement among those compared. A competing asset meeting all seven reopens that comparison. Counterparties still decide which payments to accept. [1 V3 §3.2; 2 §§2–3, 5.4–5.6]

4. Why users and enforcers can both value an exit

Paper 3 asks what happens when a counterparty defaults. If d is the probability of default, V the value at risk and r the fraction recoverable, expected unrecovered loss is d(1−r)V. At positive d and V, weaker legal recovery increases the relative appeal of independent settlement, other terms held fixed. An autonomous agent with no legal recourse faces the limiting case. An agent whose human sponsor can enforce the claim has a different payoff. Asset choice also depends on the seven properties and the counterparty’s willingness to accept payment. [3 §§2.1, 3.4, 7]

Paper 4 asks why rival authorities would sustain a common ban. A state can want to control its residents’ transfers while valuing its own ability to settle outside a rival’s reach. A member that keeps access can retain that hedge and attract capital leaving stricter jurisdictions. If a member needed for effective suppression gains by breaking ranks and can do so, the agreement is unstable. The empirical question is whether that incentive holds for a decisive member under the proposed restrictions. [4 §§2.3, 4–5, 8–9]

The answer to “eventually hacked, then zero”

A claim about a hack needs to identify what fails. Custody at an exchange, mining security, cryptographic custody and acceptance by counterparties are different things. For the cryptographic failure identified in V3, the decisive question is whether the protocol can replace what breaks. For a mining failure, it is whether mining still pays enough to secure the network. For a common ban, it is whether rivals can sustain it. The papers give concrete reasons a working network attracts miners, capital and users who need neutral settlement. They also identify conditions under which it loses those advantages. An eventual vulnerability alone establishes neither permanent loss of settlement nor a price of zero. The papers supply no probability or price target. [1 V3 §§2, 4.3, 5; 2 §§3.3, 5.1–5.6; 3 §2.1; 4 §§4–5]
