# Bitcoin Needs the Patch First

Suppose a frontier AI lab finds a flaw that could cause a critical failure in Bitcoin. The network still works. Most people do not know the flaw exists.

The lab has a head start. It can use that time to help get Bitcoin repaired. Or it can keep releasing more capable models while the weakness remains open, leaving somebody else to decide what to do with it.

The lab does not have to attack Bitcoin for the second path to end badly.

That is the thesis: frontier labs, or an independently acting AI capable of recursive self-improvement, may have enough future use for Bitcoin to pay for its defense. Bitcoin benefits only if that incentive becomes working protection before an attacker gets there.

## Two Clocks

One clock measures how long it takes to find a flaw, verify it, build a repair, and get the affected network protected. The other measures how long it takes an attacker to exploit it. Starting from the same moment, the target is:

\[
T_{\mathrm{protection}} < T_{\mathrm{attack}}
\]

A powerful private model may give defenders an early advantage. That advantage can shrink as competitors improve, public models become more capable, or researchers independently find the same weakness. There is no fixed gap between private and public capability, and no guarantee that the first finder is a defender.

Public models also help people repair software. Releasing one is not automatically an attack. The question is whether protection reaches Bitcoin faster than dangerous capability reaches someone willing and able to exploit it.

## Why Pay for the Fix?

A lab building an expensive business has something to preserve: future revenue, access to capital, customers, and the trust needed to keep expanding its data centers. A destructive attack could put those interests at risk. That gives it a reason to avoid attacking. It does not, by itself, give it a reason to fund Bitcoin security.

For that extra step, the lab needs to expect a benefit from doing the work: protecting customers, reducing a risk to its own business, or preserving a payment network it may want to use. Its expected gain from the repair must justify the bill.

An independent self-improving AI could reach a similar calculation. If its goals require continued operation, it needs electricity and hardware. While it relies on outside suppliers, Bitcoin might provide a useful payment option outside any one bank or rival's control. Keeping that option usable could be worth more than destroying it.

But Bitcoin cannot supply electricity on its own. If the AI has better payment options, little exposure to Bitcoin's failure, or no reason to keep operating, this argument weakens. Survival alone does not select Bitcoin.

## A Report Is Not a Repair

The useful sequence starts with private reporting to the relevant maintainers, followed by independent verification, a tested fix, and coordinated adoption. Public technical disclosure has to account for whether affected users can protect themselves. There is no universal safe waiting period; Bitcoin Core treats critical vulnerabilities case by case. [Security policy](https://bitcoincore.org/en/security-advisories/).

Bitcoin has already used this approach. In 2018, developers patched a critical inflation vulnerability, urged upgrades, and initially withheld its full impact while reaching affected participants. The patch release preceded full disclosure. [CVE-2018-17144](https://bitcoincore.org/en/2018/09/20/notice/).

An AI could help find the problem, prepare a verifiable patch, fund review, and support deployment. It cannot make the network safe merely by publishing code. Operators still have to upgrade; changes to consensus rules require broader coordination. A private report starts the process. It does not finish it.

If AI speeds up quantum computing, the same race applies to Bitcoin's signature cryptography. Protecting Bitcoin against a break in its signature cryptography would require an agreed migration and action by affected owners, not just an ordinary software patch. The draft [BIP 360 proposal](https://github.com/bitcoin/bips/blob/master/bip-0360.mediawiki) addresses part of that problem; it is not complete quantum protection.

## Who Picks Up the Bill?

This is the strongest objection. Everyone can benefit from a safer Bitcoin while hoping someone else pays. Labs may prioritize their own products. Maintainers may lack review capacity. A fix may arrive too late. An AI may value another network more.

Multiple powerful AIs do not solve that problem automatically. They can share an interest in Bitcoin surviving and still disagree over who contributes. One dominant AI could fund the work alone, but only if it values the result enough.

There is a real precedent for labs funding shared defense: Anthropic's Project Glasswing offers early model access and resources to organizations securing critical software. That demonstrates a funding model, not a Bitcoin-specific commitment. [Project Glasswing](https://www.anthropic.com/project/glasswing).

The evidence for this thesis would be concrete: money committed, maintainers given useful access, verified repairs, and adoption before exploitation. A lab saying it will not attack Bitcoin is a much smaller promise.

The most capable actor may prefer a working Bitcoin to a broken one. The network is protected only when that preference pays for the work—and the patch arrives first.
